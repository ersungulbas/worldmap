The most common answer is 195: the 193 member states of the United Nations plus its two observer states, the Holy See (Vatican City) and Palestine. That number is a convention, though, not a law of nature. Add places whose recognition is split, or territories that run much of their own affairs, and you'll find serious lists with well over 300 entries. Which number is "right" depends on the question you're actually asking.

## The UN count: 193 members plus two observers

The United Nations currently has 193 member states. The most recent to join was South Sudan, in July 2011. Two more states take part in the work of the General Assembly as non-member observer states: the Holy See and the State of Palestine. Neither has a vote. Palestine is recognized as a state by most UN members, while the Holy See has chosen not to seek full membership.

The Vatican is a slightly unusual case. Strictly speaking, it's the Holy See that holds the UN seat and maintains diplomatic relations, while Vatican City is the tiny city-state in Rome from which it operates. For a traveler the distinction hardly matters: if you've walked across St. Peter's Square, you've been there.

Add the two observers to the 193 members and you get 195. That's why so many atlases, quizzes and travel apps settle on that figure. It's a clean line that's easy to defend, and it's the one worldmap.tech uses for its percentage, as you'll see below.

## Places where recognition is split

Some places have their own government, borders and institutions but aren't UN members, because some countries recognize them as states and others don't. A few examples:

- **Kosovo** declared independence from Serbia in 2008. Many UN members recognize it, but not all, and it is not a UN member.
- **Taiwan** is not a UN member, and only a small number of countries formally recognize it, although it keeps unofficial ties with many more.
- **Northern Cyprus (KKTC)** declared independence in 1983 and is recognized by Türkiye.
- **Somaliland** declared independence in 1991 and is not a UN member.

Whether a list includes places like these is a judgment call, and it's where most arguments about "how many countries" start. You don't have to settle any of those arguments to count your own travels. You only need to know which list you're using.

## Territories and dependencies

Then there are places that clearly belong to, or are tied to, another country, yet feel like a separate destination when you're there. **Greenland**, the world's largest island, is an autonomous territory of the Kingdom of Denmark. **Puerto Rico** is an unincorporated territory of the United States, and Puerto Ricans have been US citizens since 1917. **Hong Kong** and **Macau** became special administrative regions of China in 1997 and 1999; each keeps its own immigration policy, issues its own passports and competes separately in international sports. The **Cook Islands** and **Niue** are in free association with New Zealand and belong to several UN agencies without being UN members.

Puerto Rico isn't a sovereign state, yet a week in San Juan feels nothing like a week in Ohio. Traveler lists exist largely to capture that difference.

## How traveler clubs count

Communities of serious country collectors deliberately go beyond the UN list. The Travelers' Century Club, which admits people who have visited at least 100 places on its list, counted 330 countries and territories as of December 2024. Most Traveled People goes much further, with roughly 1,500 regions as of 2024, and NomadMania's main list had 1,301 at the same point. The longer lists split the world into smaller regions, so "100 places" on one list and "100 countries" on another are not the same achievement.

None of these lists is more correct than the UN count. They answer a different question: not "how many sovereign states exist?" but "how much of the world have you seen?" If you're comparing numbers with someone, first check which list they use, and then how they define a visit. That second question is its own debate, covered in [what counts as visiting a country](/guides/what-counts-as-visiting-a-country/).

## How worldmap.tech counts

The map on worldmap.tech has 233 places you can tap. Alongside sovereign states, that includes Taiwan, Kosovo and Northern Cyprus, plus territories such as Greenland, Puerto Rico, Bermuda, the Faroe Islands, Hong Kong and Macau. Every place you tap adds one to your count.

The "% of the world" figure works differently. It always uses 195 as the base: the 193 UN members plus Vatican City and Palestine. Your percentage is the number of places you've marked divided by 195 and rounded, with a minimum of 1% as soon as you mark one. So 39 places is exactly 20%, and 98 places rounds to 50%. Because territories add to your count too, your number can climb past 195, while the percentage stops at 100%.

Your [traveler level](/guides/traveler-levels/) follows the same count, from Curious Traveler at your first country to Citizen of the World at 100.

## Borders and names on the map

Every world map has to pick a reference for borders and names, especially where they're disputed. On worldmap.tech, borders and country names follow the official positions of the Republic of Türkiye. In practice:

- Northern Cyprus (KKTC) is shown separately from the south of Cyprus.
- Crimea is shown as part of Ukraine, the Golan Heights as part of Syria, Western Sahara as part of Morocco and Somaliland as part of Somalia.
- Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau can each be marked separately.

The outlines come from Natural Earth, a public-domain dataset, simplified so they're easy to tap on a phone. Using one consistent reference keeps the map coherent. It's a basis for drawing the map, not a legal or official statement about any dispute.
